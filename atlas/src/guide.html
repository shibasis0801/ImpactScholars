PPMI Atlas

2026-11-18

A guide for anyone starting research with the Parkinson's Progression Markers Initiative (PPMI): the study, the neuroscience behind it, how each measurement is taken, the data and how to get it, processing by modality, the literature, the statistics, and working under the data use agreement. One research question runs through it as a worked example: is early Parkinson's disease better described by one progression dimension or two, a dopaminergic stage anchored by DaTScan and a structural stage anchored by MRI, with all six PPMI cognitive tests free to inform both? No participant-level PPMI data was read to write it; the counts it quotes come from the repository's audit notebook, run on downloads made between September and November 2026 (section 5.5).

1. The worked example at a glance

The worked example asks whether one latent disease stage explains how cognition, dopamine imaging and brain structure change in early PD, or whether two correlated stages predict later measurements better. It uses PPMI's three main longitudinal measures together, and either answer is worth publishing. The structural dimension is the hard part: PPMI's processed MRI tables are thin, so it has to be defined before it can be tested (section 6.3). In a 2026 download, 82 participants with PD untreated at enrolment had repeated measurements in all three domains, against 1,112 with repeated complete cognition (section 5.5).

The model in plain words

  • Each participant has two hidden "clocks": a dopaminergic stage D and a structural stage S. Both advance with time, and each person can sit earlier or later on each clock (a person-specific offset).

  • D is read mainly from four DaTScan binding ratios: left and right caudate and putamen.

  • S is read from T1-weighted MRI grey-matter measures. Which ones is the first decision below.

  • The six cognitive tests are not assigned in advance. Each test gets a loading on D and a loading on S, estimated from the data.

  • The one-stage model forces D and S to be the same clock. The primary test is whether the two-stage model predicts later, unseen measurements of held-out participants better.

  • The secondary question is which tests load where. The dual syndrome hypothesis (section 3.5) predicts Letter-Number Sequencing and Symbol Digit lean to D, while fluency, line orientation and delayed recall lean to S.

Decisions to make, in order

  1. Define S. Either use the processed total grey matter volume: one number per scan at baseline, 24 and 48 months, repeated for 137 participants in a 2026 download, with no head-size correction or quality flags. Or reprocess the raw T1 scans into regional measures: about 327 participants had 3D T1 at two or more main visits, but processing takes weeks. PPMI's FreeSurfer tables do not settle it: in 2026 they had baseline only (section 5.5).

  2. Fix the cohort and the time axis. Which cohort codes count as "PD, untreated at entry"? Is the follow-up window 5 years or 5.5? Is time measured from baseline or from diagnosis? Dates exist only to the month.

  3. Decide how to handle treatment. Almost everyone starts dopaminergic drugs during follow-up. Options: levodopa-equivalent dose as a time-varying covariate, medication state at testing, and sensitivity analyses.

  4. Specify the model. One offset per person, or one per dimension? Person-specific rates of progression, or shared rates? Linear or sigmoid trajectories? What constraints make D and S identifiable?

  5. Fix the evaluation before fitting. Primary metric (held-out negative log-likelihood), the 2.5-year landmark, nested participant-level cross-validation, and a written decision rule.

  6. Derive every count in one notebook with written definitions, and freeze one cohort table before any number goes public.

  7. Settle the small choices that change results: JLO_TOTRAW rather than JLO_TOTCALC (which is exactly twice the raw score), how screening and baseline DaTScans align, and never pooling the two DaTScan quantification methods.

Read it from the middle outwards. Each scan anchors one stage, and every test receives one arrow from D and one from S; the data set how strong each arrow is. The one-stage model in the bottom band removes the distinction by giving each person a single offset.

2. PPMI

PPMI is the Michael J. Fox Foundation's observational, longitudinal, multicentre natural-history study of PD, launched in 2010. Since 2026 it is called the Parkinson's Precision Medicine Initiative, with the same acronym. Approved researchers get its data free through LONI IDA, under a Data Use Agreement (DUA) that bars AI agents from individual-level data.

2.1 Design and history

  • Start. Enrolment began on 1 June 2010. The original baseline cohort was 423 untreated PD, 196 healthy controls and 64 SWEDD participants at 24 sites (Marek et al. 2018).

  • Expansion ("PPMI 2.0"). A new protocol started on 1 July 2020 (NCT04477785). It runs at about 50 sites in 12 countries, targets roughly 4,000 to 4,500 clinic participants, and runs to 2033.

  • Three collections (Data User Guide, Aug 2026):

    • PPMI Clinical: in-person visits with every modality.

    • PPMI Remote: smell tests and some genetics, aiming at 40,000 people.

    • PPMI Online: self-reported questionnaires, aiming at 100,000.

  • Related studies. Sub-study 004 adds DaTscan and AV-133 PET (which images VMAT2) in about 50 early PD and 100 prodromal participants. The Path to Prevention platform trial uses PPMI's infrastructure for prodromal PD.

2.3 Visits and the schedule

  • Visit codes (EVENT_ID, sometimes CLINICAL_EVENT):

    • SC: screening, typically about 60 days before baseline.

    • BL: baseline, the formal study entry.

    • V01, V02 and onwards: scheduled follow-ups.

    • R codes: remote assessments.

    • U codes: unscheduled visits.

    • LOG: running logs such as medications.

    • Older data also has ST (start of symptomatic therapy) and PW (premature withdrawal).

  • Dates are rounded to the month to protect privacy. That is why this repository's pipeline works at month precision.

  • Visit codes are not fully reliable. The User Guide says best practice for using EVENT_ID in longitudinal analysis is "under review". Use the dates for time, and the codes only to join records from the same visit.

  • The original schedule (Marek et al. 2018):

    • Motor exam every 3 months in year 1, then every 6 months.

    • Cognitive battery yearly.

    • DaTscan in PD at screening, 12, 24 and 48 months.

    • MRI at baseline for everyone, repeated in a subset.

    • CSF at baseline, 6 and 12 months, then yearly.

  • Common code-to-month mapping in original-protocol data: V04 = 12, V06 = 24, V08 = 36, V10 = 48 and V12 = 60 months. The grey matter volume visits (BL, V06, V10) match this. Confirm against the schedule of activities before relying on any code.

2.4 Imaging

DaTscan SPECT (SPECT Imaging Procedure Manual, Jan 2025; Marek et al. 2018):

  • Preparation and dose. A thyroid-blocking agent is given first. The dose is 111 to 185 MBq (3 to 5 mCi) of iodine-123 ioflupane.

  • Acquisition. Imaging starts 4 hours (plus or minus 30 minutes) after injection: 120 projections, 128 x 128 matrix.

  • Original pipeline. Reconstruction and attenuation correction were done centrally, then images were normalised to a template in standard (MNI) space.

  • The ratio. Specific binding ratio (SBR) = target region / reference region - 1. The original reference region is the occipital cortex.

  • Core lab change. The imaging core lab moved from Invicro to XingImaging in November 2024. This is why there are two SBR tables. The archived Invicro analysis uses the occipital reference, and the Xing Core Lab table uses cerebral white matter (its columns end in _REF_CWM). Never pool them.

  • Other tracers. The manual also allows a technetium tracer (TRODAT-1) at some sites. This repository's pipeline excludes non-ioflupane scans.

MRI (MRI Procedure Manual, Jan 2025; Marek et al. 2018):

  • Original protocol. Only 10 sites ran a standardised protocol on 3 T Siemens Trio scanners (3D MPRAGE plus DTI). Other sites used their own scanners, at 1.5 or 3 T.

  • Current protocol:

    • 3D T1-weighted (MP-RAGE or IR-FSPGR): 1 mm sagittal slices, 192 slices, 256 mm field of view, about 7 minutes.

    • Resting-state fMRI.

    • Neuromelanin-sensitive imaging (NM-MT), which images the substantia nigra directly.

    • DTI.

    • 3D T2 FLAIR.

  • Scanner consistency. Each site must keep the same scanner and parameters over time.

  • Derived data in IDA. IDA distributes a grey matter volume table (one total per scan) and Xing MRI acquisition metadata. IDA also has regional FreeSurfer 7 tables (ASEG volumes and APARC cortical thickness); check which visits they cover before relying on them.

2.5 Clinical and biospecimen data

MDS-UPDRS, the cognitive battery and many non-motor scales are collected at visits (section 4). Medications are in LEDD_Concomitant_Medication_Log as levodopa-equivalent daily dose (LEDD). Biospecimens include:

  • CSF alpha-synuclein seed amplification assay (SAA). Siderowf et al. 2023 reported sensitivity of 87.7% for PD and specificity of 96.3% against controls. Sensitivity was 93.3% in sporadic PD but only 67.5% in LRRK2 carriers.

  • CSF Alzheimer markers: amyloid-beta 42, total tau and p-tau181.

  • Neurofilament light in serum and CSF.

  • Genetics: genetic status tables in Study Data (pathogenic variants such as LRRK2, GBA and SNCA, and APOE genotype), and whole-genome sequencing under Genetic Data.

  • Other: urine, skin and digital sensor data.

2.6 Getting and using the data

  • Access. Apply on LONI IDA. Name everyone who will use the data; each must register and accept the DUA. Update the application yearly.

  • Data tiers. Tier 1 is public and refreshed daily. Tier 2 is embargoed or needs a request form. Tier 3 needs approval from the Data Access Committee.

  • The data changes daily and is not curated, so results differ between downloads. Record your download date: the publication statement needs it.

  • The Curated Data Cut. This is one merged Excel table, PPMI_Curated_Data_Cut_Public_YYYYMMDD; the January 2024 extract has 3,096 participants and 160 columns. It includes cohort and subgroup, NSD stage, LEDD, the cognitive tests, investigator cognitive diagnosis, MDS-UPDRS (on and off medication), progression milestones, CSF markers, NfL and DaTscan SBRs. Use it to cross-check your own pipeline. notebooks/atlas.ipynb downloads it (section 5.2).

  • The AI rule (DUA version 5.0, April 2026, paragraph 3 and Appendix A):

    • The appendix states that "the application of AI agents to individual-level PPMI data is prohibited".

    • Uploading PPMI data to a third-party platform that retains or trains on user content is also a violation. An opt-out clause is not enough.

    • Check brainlife.io and any cloud service against this before using them (section 6.8).

    • AI tools are fine on code, documentation and synthetic data.

  • Publishing (Publication Policy, Sept 2024). A paper that uses PPMI data counts as an "External Publication":

    • Submit it to the Data Use and Publications Committee through the PPMI website before journal submission. The administrative review takes two weeks.

    • Include the data-source statement with the download date and RRID:SCR_006431, plus the funding statement listing all funding partners.

    • Notify the committee when the paper is published.

    • Posting a preprint, a CC BY licence and depositing the code are strongly encouraged.

3. The neuroscience

Parkinson's disease (PD) is a progressive alpha-synuclein disorder. Its core motor signs come from losing dopamine neurons that project from the substantia nigra to the putamen. Its cognitive decline has at least two sources: frontostriatal dopamine loss, and spreading cortical and cholinergic pathology. That second point is the biological case for the worked example's two-dimension model.

3.1 What goes wrong

  • The protein. Alpha-synuclein misfolds and aggregates into Lewy bodies (in cell bodies) and Lewy neurites (in axons and dendrites). Affected neurons dysfunction and die. A seed amplification assay (SAA) can now detect misfolded alpha-synuclein in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) in living people.

  • The spread (Braak et al. 2003). Autopsy studies suggest a typical sequence in six stages:

    1. Dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus and the olfactory bulb.

    2. Lower brainstem, including the locus coeruleus (noradrenaline) and raphe nuclei (serotonin).

    3. Midbrain, including the substantia nigra pars compacta (dopamine). Motor symptoms usually begin around here.

    4. Temporal mesocortex and allocortex (transentorhinal region, hippocampus).

    5. High-order association neocortex, including prefrontal cortex.

    6. First-order association and premotor cortex, sometimes primary areas.

  • Two routes in (Borghammer & Van Den Berge 2019; Horsager et al. 2020). In "body-first" PD, pathology starts in the gut or autonomic nerves and climbs the vagus. These patients often have REM sleep behaviour disorder (RBD) and autonomic signs years before motor signs, and more symmetric dopamine loss. In "brain-first" PD, pathology starts in the brain (perhaps the olfactory bulb or amygdala), with more asymmetric dopamine loss and fewer prodromal signs. This is one source of the heterogeneity any progression model must absorb.

  • The threshold. Motor signs appear only after much of the nigrostriatal system is gone. Fearnley & Lees (1991) estimated that about 68% of neurons in the worst-hit (ventrolateral) tier, and about half of the caudal nigra, are gone at symptom onset. At autopsy, putamen dopamine is almost entirely depleted, while parts of the caudate keep up to about 40% (Kish et al. 1988).

  • Axons go first (Cheng et al. 2010; Kordower et al. 2013). Dopamine terminal markers in the dorsal putamen are sparse within a few years of diagnosis and almost absent by about four years. Nigral cell bodies are lost more slowly. For the worked example this matters a great deal: putamen DaTScan signal falls fast early and then hits a floor, so it carries little information about later stages.

Caveat: Braak staging comes from cross-sectional autopsies, and not every brain follows it. Treat it as a common pattern, not a law.

3.2 The nigrostriatal dopamine system

The substantia nigra pars compacta (SNc) sits in the ventral midbrain. Its dopamine neurons project to the striatum, which is the caudate nucleus plus the putamen. The neighbouring ventral tegmental area (VTA) projects to the ventral striatum (nucleus accumbens) and to prefrontal cortex.

The loss has a map. The ventrolateral tier of the SNc is lost first and most (Fearnley & Lees 1991). It projects to the dorsal and posterior putamen, the motor territory. The caudate (associative territory) and ventral striatum (limbic) are relatively spared early. Three things follow, and you will see all of them in DaTScan data:

  • The putamen has the lowest binding ratio, the caudate higher.

  • Binding falls from posterior to anterior along the striatum.

  • The side opposite the more affected limbs shows lower binding, because each hemisphere controls the opposite body side.

The synapse, step by step. Tyrosine hydroxylase turns tyrosine into L-DOPA, and aromatic amino acid decarboxylase turns L-DOPA into dopamine. VMAT2 packs dopamine into vesicles. After release, the dopamine transporter (DAT) pulls it back into the terminal. Receptors are D1-like (D1, D5; excitatory signalling) or D2-like (D2, D3, D4; inhibitory signalling).

What DaTScan sees. The tracer binds DAT on presynaptic terminals, so the signal indexes the density of surviving dopamine terminals. Early in disease, surviving neurons may downregulate DAT to keep more dopamine in the synapse. DaTScan can therefore slightly overstate terminal loss early on.

3.3 The basal ganglia circuits

The basal ganglia select and scale actions and thoughts. They act through loops that run cortex to striatum to output nuclei to thalamus and back to cortex. Dopamine sets the balance inside these loops.

Parts: the striatum (caudate, putamen, nucleus accumbens), globus pallidus external (GPe) and internal (GPi) segments, the subthalamic nucleus (STN), and the substantia nigra (pars compacta, which supplies dopamine; pars reticulata, which is an output nucleus like GPi). Thalamic nuclei relay the output back to cortex.

Pathways (Albin et al. 1989; DeLong 1990):

  • Direct. Cortex excites striatal neurons that carry D1 receptors. They inhibit GPi and SNr, which releases the thalamus and facilitates the selected action.

  • Indirect. Striatal neurons with D2 receptors inhibit GPe. That releases the STN, which drives GPi and SNr to inhibit the thalamus and suppress competing actions.

  • Hyperdirect. Cortex drives the STN directly, a fast brake (Nambu et al. 2002).

  • Dopamine excites the direct pathway and inhibits the indirect one, so its net effect favours movement. Losing it leaves GPi overactive and the thalamus and cortex underdriven: slowness and poverty of movement (bradykinesia).

This "rate model" explains a lot, but not everything. Modern accounts add abnormally synchronised beta-band (13 to 30 Hz) oscillations in the loops. Deep brain stimulation of the STN or GPi works partly by disrupting them.

3.4 Beyond dopamine

  • Acetylcholine. The nucleus basalis of Meynert (NBM) supplies cholinergic input to the whole cortex. It degenerates in PD, and cortical cholinergic loss is worse in PD dementia than in Alzheimer's disease (Bohnen et al. 2003). Lower NBM volume on MRI predicted later cognitive impairment in early PD (Schulz et al. 2018). The pedunculopontine nucleus, another cholinergic group, relates to gait and falls.

  • Noradrenaline. The locus coeruleus is hit early (Braak stage 2). It supports arousal and attention, and its loss is linked to depression and cognitive slowing.

  • Serotonin. Raphe nuclei degenerate early too, with links to depression and anxiety.

  • Cortex and co-pathology. Lewy pathology reaches the cortex late. Many patients also carry Alzheimer's pathology (amyloid plaques, tau tangles), which adds to dementia risk; low CSF amyloid-beta 42 predicts faster cognitive decline.

  • What MRI can see. Early PD shows only subtle grey-matter change: mild cortical thinning and subcortical atrophy, more in patients with mild cognitive impairment. Network-level atrophy patterns (Zeighami et al. 2015) and NBM volume carry more signal than global measures. Total grey matter volume mostly reflects age and head size, a problem for a structural anchor (section 9).

Dosing for the depleted dorsal striatum can "overdose" the relatively intact ventral striatum and impair flexible learning (Gotham et al. 1988; Cools 2006). Because PPMI participants start treatment during follow-up, medication shifts the frontostriatal tests over time.

3.6 The clinical picture in one paragraph

Diagnosis requires bradykinesia plus rest tremor or rigidity, with supportive and exclusion criteria (Postuma et al. 2015). Non-motor signs often come first: loss of smell, RBD, constipation, depression, anxiety and low blood pressure on standing (Postuma & Berg 2016). Severity is rated with the Hoehn and Yahr stages (1967) and the MDS-UPDRS (Goetz et al. 2008). Treatment is levodopa, dopamine agonists or MAO-B inhibitors, summarised as a levodopa-equivalent daily dose (Jost et al. 2023).

Each is capped at plus or minus 3, and the CSS is their equal-weight mean. The paper validated it at baseline only.

4.3 DaTScan SPECT

  1. Thyroid block. A blocking agent such as potassium iodide stops free radioactive iodine collecting in the thyroid.

  2. Injection. 111 to 185 MBq of iodine-123 ioflupane. The tracer crosses into the brain and binds the dopamine transporter on dopamine terminals.

  3. Wait. Four hours, so that binding reaches a quasi-steady state and background clears.

  4. Acquisition. Gamma cameras rotate around the head and count 159 keV photons from 120 angles.

  5. Reconstruction. An iterative algorithm turns the projections into a 3D image. Attenuation correction allows for photons absorbed by the skull and tissue.

  6. Quantification. The image is aligned to a template. Counts are averaged in caudate, putamen and a reference region with no specific binding. Then SBR = target / reference - 1.

  7. Visual read. A normal striatum looks like a comma on each side. Loss of putamen signal turns it into a full stop. SWEDD participants had a normal read despite a PD diagnosis.

What moves the SBR besides disease:

  • Age. Binding falls in healthy ageing, by about 8% per decade in an early SPECT study (van Dyck et al. 1995), fairly evenly across caudate and putamen (van Dyck et al. 2002).

  • Sex. Women have higher binding on average.

  • Medicines acting on monoamine transporters, such as some antidepressants and stimulants.

  • Reference region and method. Occipital and white-matter references give different numbers.

  • Equipment. Camera, collimator and reconstruction settings.

  • Partial volume. The striatum is small relative to SPECT resolution of about 1 cm. Atrophy and head motion blur the signal.

Levodopa itself has at most a small effect on DaTscan binding at usual doses, but treatment changes which participants keep coming to visits.

4.4 Structural MRI

  • What a T1-weighted image shows. Contrast separates grey matter, white matter and cerebrospinal fluid. PPMI's 3D T1 has 1 mm voxels, about 16 million of them in a 256 mm cube.

  • Volumes. Software labels each voxel by tissue or brain region and sums the voxels. Cortical thickness is measured on reconstructed cortical surfaces.

  • What changes measured volume besides disease:

    • Head size, so you must adjust for total intracranial volume (ICV).

    • Age (roughly 0.2 to 0.5% loss per year in later life) and sex.

    • Scanner, field strength, coil and software upgrades.

    • Head motion, which mimics atrophy.

    • The processing software and its version.

  • Why total grey matter volume is a weak anchor. It mixes every region, so small focal disease effects drown in age and head-size variance. It also comes without ICV or quality flags.

  • Other sequences. DTI measures water diffusion (fractional anisotropy, mean diffusivity, free water). Neuromelanin MRI shows the pigmented dopamine neurons of the substantia nigra directly. Resting-state fMRI measures correlated activity between regions.

4.5 Biospecimens

  • CSF comes from a lumbar puncture. The SAA mixes the sample with recombinant alpha-synuclein and cycles shaking and incubation. Misfolded seeds template aggregation, which a fluorescent dye (thioflavin T) reports. The result is positive, negative or inconclusive, not a level.

  • Amyloid-beta 42, total tau and p-tau181 are measured by immunoassay. Low amyloid-beta 42 signals Alzheimer co-pathology.

  • Neurofilament light is a protein released by damaged axons. It is measurable in blood and tracks the rate of neurodegeneration, but it is not disease-specific.

4.6 Properties of these measures that matter for modelling

  • Practice effects. Scores rise on retesting, especially in year 1, and can mask decline. Avila Pérez et al. 2025 found smaller one-year practice effects predicted later impairment.

  • Ceilings and floors. Line orientation and MoCA crowd near the top in early PD, and the putamen SBR crowds near the bottom after a few years. Ceilings and floors make a score less informative and make its errors non-normal.

  • Medication state. It changes motor scores and some cognitive scores. Record it and model it.

  • Different schedules. Cognition is yearly, DaTscan at screening, 12, 24 and 48 months, and processed MRI at baseline, 24 and 48 months. The model must handle measurements that never coincide.

  • Missingness is not random. Sicker participants miss visits and withdraw. Ignoring this biases trajectories towards healthier people (section 10).

5.6 Caveats to keep in mind

  • Screening vs baseline. DaTScan was done at screening (SC), while cognition and MRI start at baseline (BL), about two months later. Align them by date, not by code.

  • Two SBR methods. Xing (white matter reference) and archived Invicro (occipital reference) values are on different scales. Model them separately or harmonise them explicitly.

  • Grey matter volume is one number per scan. It has no head-size measure, no quality flags and no record of the software used. It cannot define S regionally.

  • A T1 acquisition flag is not usable data. It says a scan exists, not that it passed quality control or was processed.

  • Month-level dates. An MRI and the cognitive tests of the same visit can fall in different months. Match them by visit and check the gap.

  • Test scales. Use JLO_TOTRAW (0 to 15), the scale of the CSS norms. JLO_TOTCALC doubles it.

  • CSS limits. The CSS uses the published norms; IDA codes SEX 0 and 1 map to the paper's codes 2 and 1. The paper validated it only at baseline, so later visits are exploratory.

  • Exports drift. PPMI updates daily. Keep each download separate and quote its date with every number.

  • The DUA boundary. Participant rows, DuckDB files and notebook outputs stay in the approved environment and never go into an AI tool. Only aggregate tables leave it.

  • Not every download loads as a table. The Curated Data Cut is an Excel workbook: data.PPMI lists it but does not load it, so read it with pandas.read_excel(path, engine="calamine") after installing python-calamine (section 6.5). A table that is not UTF-8 is read as Latin-1, and p.notes says so.

  • IDA names can drift. The catalogue matches downloads by file name. If a table you downloaded still shows as missing, atlas.ipynb lists it among the files not in the catalogue; write its name without the date in the file column of code/catalog.csv.

6. Processing and analysis by modality

Each modality needs its own cleaning and measurement model before it can enter a joint model. The hard part is not any single modality. It is putting all three on one timeline without leaking information between training and test participants. Sections 6.1 to 6.4 cover that modelling. The work itself starts earlier, with preprocessing: turning raw downloads (study tables, images, genetic files) into analysis tables. Sections 6.5 to 6.8 cover it, with free tools and platforms for every step.

6.1 Cognition

  1. Validate. Check ranges, duplicate records and test versions. This repository's pipeline already does this.

  2. Norm. Convert raw scores to demographically adjusted z-scores: the observed score minus the score expected from control data for that age, sex and education, divided by the control residual spread. This is how the CSS works. You can use the published norms or fit norms on PPMI's own controls; css.py supports both.

  3. Model practice effects. Scores rise on retesting, especially at the first repeat. Give each observation a "number of previous administrations" term, and let control participants' trajectories (ageing plus practice) set the reference.

  4. Keep tests separate where possible. An equal-weight composite throws away exactly the information the worked example needs: whether tests move differently. The two-dimension model gives each test its own loadings.

  5. Check that tests mean the same thing over time (measurement invariance). If a test's relationship to the latent stage changes between visits, latent scores at different visits are not comparable.

  6. Respect the score's shape. Line orientation is a count out of 15, crowded at the ceiling, so model it as binomial or ordinal rather than normal. Recall counts are bounded too.

  7. Handle motor and medication effects. Run sensitivity analyses that adjust SDMT for MDS-UPDRS III or drop it. Record medication state at testing and LEDD.

  8. Define clinical endpoints. Candidates: mild cognitive impairment by MDS Level I criteria (Litvan et al. 2012), the investigator's cognitive diagnosis, time to dementia, and PPMI's progression milestones.

  9. Individual change. If you ever classify a single person as "declined", use a reliable change index (Jacobson & Truax 1991) or regression-based change norms, not raw differences.

6.2 DaTScan

  1. Pick one quantification series per analysis. Use Xing (white matter reference) for current data and the Invicro series (occipital reference) for legacy analyses. If you must combine them, estimate the method difference on scans quantified both ways and say how.

  2. Choose regional summaries:

    • Caudate and putamen, left and right.

    • Mean striatum.

    • The putamen-to-caudate ratio, which captures the posterior-first pattern.

    • An asymmetry index: (left - right) / mean.

    • Values on the side opposite the more affected limbs ("contralateral"), which carry the most signal early.

  3. Adjust for age and sex. Express each SBR as a percentage of the value expected for a healthy person of the same age and sex, from control data. PPMI's biological staging uses this: "D positive" means the lowest putamen value is below 75% of expected.

  4. Use a sensible shape over time. Loss is fastest early and slows as the putamen approaches its floor, roughly exponential decay. Model log(SBR), or a curve that flattens towards an asymptote. Expect the caudate to carry most of the information about later stages.

  5. Time. Use the scan date relative to the baseline date. Screening scans sit about two months before baseline.

  6. Measurement error. Repeat scans at one visit are rare, so take test-retest error from the literature or estimate it as a residual variance in the model.

  7. Raw images allow voxel-wise and sub-regional analyses (for example the posterior putamen), but that needs SPECT image processing, a project of its own. Section 6.6 sets out the steps.

6.3 Structural MRI

Option A: the processed grey matter volume table (137 participants with GMV at two or more visits in 2026; section 5.5). Model within-person change over time, which is much less affected by head size than between-person differences. Adjust for age and sex and check units and outliers. Present S built on it as exploratory.

Option B: process the raw T1 scans (about 327 participants had 3D T1 at two or more main visits in 2026). PPMI's FreeSurfer tables had baseline only, so change over time needs this route. It is the route to a credible structural stage:

  1. Download. Get the 3D T1 series from IDA's image collections as DICOM, choosing one consistent series type per participant. ida.fetch downloads a collection from its Advanced Download URL list (step 4 of notebooks/atlas.ipynb).

  2. Convert and organise. Convert to NIfTI with dcm2niix (Li et al. 2016) and organise the files in BIDS layout (Gorgolewski et al. 2016). Section 6.6 covers these steps for every image type.

  3. Quality-control the inputs. Use MRIQC (Esteban et al. 2017) for automated metrics plus a visual check for motion, artefacts and incomplete coverage.

  4. Segment with one of these tools:

    • FreeSurfer's longitudinal stream (Fischl 2012; Reuter et al. 2012), the standard for within-person change in volume and thickness.

    • FastSurfer (Henschel et al. 2020), a much faster deep-learning version.

    • SynthSeg+ (Billot et al. 2023), which is robust to scanner differences.

    • CAT12 (Gaser et al. 2024), for voxel-based morphometry in SPM.

  5. Extract the measures:

    • Intracranial volume.

    • Thalamus, hippocampus, amygdala, caudate and putamen volumes.

    • Regional cortical thickness, especially temporal and parietal.

    • Optionally, nucleus basalis volume from a basal forebrain atlas (as in Schulz et al. 2018) and deformation-based network measures (Zeighami et al. 2015).

  6. Harmonise across scanners. Include site and scanner as covariates, or use ComBat (Johnson et al. 2007; Fortin et al. 2018) or longitudinal ComBat (Beer et al. 2020). Fit the harmonisation inside each training fold.

  7. Quality-control the outputs. Look for failed segmentations, implausible yearly changes and outlier participants.

  8. Freeze the variables before linking them to cognition. Cognition must not choose the regions. Write the list down and timestamp it.

  9. Budget the compute. FreeSurfer takes hours per scan, and about 1,000 scans means thousands of CPU hours. Use institutional computing that satisfies the DUA; a third-party platform that retains data does not.

Option C: diffusion and neuromelanin. Free water in the posterior substantia nigra rose over four years in a multi-site study of early PD, with no change in controls (Burciu et al. 2017). With 442 participants having two or more DTI visits, it is a stronger progression marker than total grey matter. Neuromelanin MRI is in the current protocol but has little longitudinal coverage yet.

6.4 Putting the modalities on one timeline

  • Time axis. Use years since baseline, from dates. Diagnosis can precede baseline by up to two years, so a latent time model estimates each person's offset relative to a shared disease time instead.

  • Asynchrony is fine. In a joint model with a shared latent stage, every observation enters at its own time. Visits do not need to coincide, and forcing them to ("same-visit" filters) throws data away.

  • Missing data. Likelihood-based models are valid when missingness depends only on observed data ("missing at random"). Dropout after decline violates that, so plan a sensitivity analysis (section 10).

  • Covariates: baseline age, sex, education, LEDD over time, site or scanner, test form and practice.

  • No leakage. Every fitted transformation must be estimated on training participants only, inside each cross-validation fold. That covers norms, z-scoring, harmonisation, scaling and imputation (Kaufman et al. 2011).

  • Landmark prediction. For held-out participants, estimate their stages from data up to about 2.5 years, then predict their later measurements. This mirrors how a score would be used in a trial (van Houwelingen 2007).

6.5 Preprocessing: from raw downloads to tables

Preprocessing turns what IDA sends into tables a model can use. Study tables arrive nearly ready and need typing, decoding and a few derived scores. Images arrive as scanner files and need the most work (section 6.6). Genetic files need only the few variants a hypothesis names. Every tool named here is free (section 6.7).

A finished table has:

  • Keys on every row: PATNO, EVENT_ID (CLINICAL_EVENT in biospecimen tables) and the date (to the month, as PPMI gives it), plus the image ID for anything measured on a scan, which links it back to the image search.

  • One meaning per column: numbers stored as numbers, codes decoded, units in the column name or a units column, and missing values as NULL rather than special codes.

  • Provenance: the source file and its release date, the tool and version that made each value, and a quality flag.

  • A place outside the repository. Keep derived tables in the DuckDB connection next to the raw ones, or in DuckDB or Parquet files outside the repository (p.export refuses paths inside it). They are still participant-level data under the DUA (section 11.4).

Rules for every pipeline:

  • Raw files are read-only. Keep each download as IDA sent it, and write every output somewhere else.

  • Script every step from raw file to final table, so a new data release reruns end to end.

  • Pin versions. Record each tool's version, and run imaging tools from their published containers so every scan is processed the same way.

  • Count at every step: rows in, rows out, and why any were dropped.

  • Fit nothing on the whole data set yet. Preprocessing converts and measures. Norms, harmonisation, scaling and imputation are estimated later, inside each cross-validation fold (section 6.4).

The tables that need the most work:

  • Coded values. Load the Code List and the Data Dictionary (CSV files under Study Docs) as tables, and join on table and column names to decode any value. In the Data Dictionary, MOD_NAME is the table and ITM_NAME the column. Some fields hold codes for "not done" or "unable to rate". The Code List names them, so turn them into NULL before summing anything.

  • MDS-UPDRS. Recompute each part's total from its items and compare it with the total supplied. Follow the published rules for how many items may be missing before a total is invalid (Goetz et al. 2015). Part III can be recorded off and on medication at the same visit. PDSTATE says which, so keep the two exams apart and never average them. The tremor-dominant and PIGD subtypes come from the ratio of the mean of 11 tremor items to the mean of 5 gait and balance items: 1.15 or more is tremor-dominant, 0.90 or less is PIGD, and anything between is indeterminate (Stebbins et al. 2013).

  • LEDD. Each row of the medication log is one drug with its levodopa-equivalent daily dose, a start month and, once stopped, a stop month. For each visit, add up the drugs active on the visit date. Follow PPMI's "Method for calculating Levodopa Equivalent Dose" (Study Docs) and the conversion factors of Jost et al. 2023. COMT inhibitors add a fraction of the levodopa dose rather than a fixed amount, so compute them after summing levodopa. Dates are months only, so write down how you treat a drug that starts in the visit month. Check your totals against the LEDD in the Curated Data Cut.

  • Biospecimens. Results come in long format: PATNO, CLINICAL_EVENT (the visit), TESTNAME and the value as text. The same analyte can come from several projects and kits, and some values lie below or above an assay's detection limit. Pick one project or assay per analyte. Keep a censored value as its limit plus a flag, not as zero or missing. Harmonise units, log-transform skewed analytes such as NfL, then pivot to one column per analyte. SAA results are categories (positive, negative, inconclusive), not levels.

  • Curated Data Cut. pandas.read_excel(path, sheet_name=None, engine="calamine") reads every sheet once python-calamine is installed, and p.save("curated_data_cut", frame) adds a sheet to the connection as a table. Use it to check your own derived values, and as the source of its curated endpoints (section 7).

  • Genetics. APOE genotype and pathogenic-variant status already arrive as tables, one row per participant. For MAPT and COMT, extract only the variants a hypothesis names from the whole-genome VCF files, with PLINK 2 (Chang et al. 2015) or BCFtools (Danecek et al. 2021): the MAPT H1/H2 haplotype from a tagging variant such as rs1052553 (allele A marks H1, G marks H2), and COMT Val158Met from rs4680. Write allele counts per participant, and check how the files' sample IDs map to PATNO. Do not call GBA variants from short-read data yourself: a nearby pseudogene makes that error-prone, so use PPMI's genetic status table.

  • Digital sensors. The watch and app data are features computed per day or per task, not raw signals. Summarise each feature over a fixed window around each visit, for example the median over the four weeks after it. Require a minimum number of valid days, and keep that number as a column.

  • Online and remote questionnaires are keyed by PATNO, not by clinic visit. Align them to visits by date, within a window you fix in advance.

6.6 Preprocessing images

PPMI's derived tables (DaTscan SBRs, grey matter volume, FreeSurfer, DTI regions) answer many questions without opening an image. Process raw images when you need a measure those tables lack, such as regional MRI change over time, sub-regions of the striatum, nigral free water or neuromelanin. Every image type shares the first steps:

  1. Choose and download. Save an image search as CSV, which loads as the images table, and choose one consistent series type per participant. ida.fetch downloads a collection from its Advanced Download URL list.

  2. Link each series to its visit. IDA writes the image ID (I followed by digits) into the download's folder names. image_files reads it, and joining it to the images table gives PATNO and visit. PPMI's derived tables carry an IMAGEID column too, so check how each writes the ID before joining.

  3. Convert DICOM to compressed NIfTI with dcm2niix (Li et al. 2016). Beside each image it writes a JSON file with the scanner's make, model and field strength and the sequence settings. Collect these into a scans table, because they are the covariates for harmonisation. For diffusion it also writes the b-values and gradient directions.

  4. Organise the files in BIDS layout (Gorgolewski et al. 2016) with HeuDiConv (Halchenko et al. 2024) or dcm2bids, and check the result with the BIDS Validator. Use PATNO as the subject and the visit code as the session (for example sub-3001/ses-BL), so every output joins back without a lookup table.

  5. Check the inputs. Run MRIQC (Esteban et al. 2017) on the MRI; PPMI also distributes an MRIQC table. Look at every SPECT and PET image too, in FSLeyes or ITK-SNAP, for motion, truncation and missing slices. Record a pass or fail per series, with the reason.

  6. Process each modality with its recipe below, using one version of each tool for every scan.

  7. Extract regional values into a long table (image ID, region, measure, value), join it to PATNO and visit, and load it next to the study tables.

  8. Check the outputs. Look for failed registrations and segmentations, implausible yearly changes and outlying participants. Compare with PPMI's derived table wherever one exists.

DaTscan SPECT. Process raw scans only for what the SBR tables lack, such as sub-regions of the putamen or voxel-wise maps.

  • Start from reconstructed images. IDA holds both raw and reconstructed DaTscan series, and the series description says which. PPMI's DaTscan methods document describes the core lab's reconstruction and attenuation correction.

  • Register to standard space. SPECT shows little anatomy, so register it to a DaTscan template rather than a T1 template: a published one, or one built from healthy controls' scans with ANTs (Avants et al. 2011). When the same visit has a T1, you can instead register the SPECT to the T1 and reuse the T1's transform to standard space. SPM's spatial normalisation is the classic route.

  • Apply regions. Take left and right caudate and putamen, and the reference region, from an atlas in the same space, such as the Harvard-Oxford atlases that nilearn (Abraham et al. 2014) downloads. Use cerebral white matter as the reference to compare with the Xing values, or occipital cortex to compare with the archived Invicro ones. For a posterior-putamen measure, split the putamen front to back.

  • Compute SBR = target mean / reference mean - 1 for each region with nibabel (section 4.3), into a table keyed by image ID.

  • Validate against the core lab. For the same image IDs, your SBRs should track PPMI's closely. Never pool the two series; say which one you used.

Structural MRI (3D T1). Option B in section 6.3 sets out the route. FreeSurfer's longitudinal stream (Reuter et al. 2012) or FastSurfer (Henschel et al. 2020) gives the volumes and thicknesses, and SynthSeg+ (Billot et al. 2023) is robust to scanner differences. FreeSurfer's asegstats2table and aparcstats2table turn the outputs into one row per scan, including intracranial volume (eTIV); FastSurfer writes its statistics in the same format.

Diffusion MRI. QSIPrep (Cieslak et al. 2021) runs denoising, motion and eddy-current correction and alignment to the T1 as one BIDS app. Fit the diffusion tensor with DIPY (Garyfallidis et al. 2014) or MRtrix3 (Tournier et al. 2019), then average fractional anisotropy and mean diffusivity within regions. Free water in the posterior substantia nigra, the progression marker of section 6.3, comes from a two-compartment model (Pasternak et al. 2009). Its regularised form works on single-shell data, at the cost of stronger assumptions; DIPY's free-water model needs two or more non-zero b-values, so read the b-values before choosing. FSL (Jenkinson et al. 2012) has the classic alternatives, such as eddy and TBSS.

Resting-state fMRI (a sub-study from 2012 to 2019). fMRIPrep (Esteban et al. 2019) preprocesses it, and nilearn extracts regional time series from an atlas and builds connectivity matrices. Head motion matters most here: keep fMRIPrep's framewise displacement, and exclude high-motion runs by a rule fixed in advance.

Neuromelanin MRI. Register to a template with ANTs, then compare the substantia nigra with a nearby reference region (contrast ratio = (nigra - reference) / reference), or measure the volume of bright voxels. Few participants have repeat scans yet.

PET (AV-133, tau). Register each PET image to a T1 from the same or nearest visit, segment the T1 with FreeSurfer, and compute standardised uptake value ratios (SUVR = target mean / reference mean), using the reference region the sub-study's methods name. PETSurfer, part of FreeSurfer (Greve et al. 2014), does this with partial-volume correction.

Where to run it. Table steps and SPECT run on a laptop. FreeSurfer (which PETSurfer also needs), QSIPrep and fMRIPrep take hours per scan, so run them as containers on institutional computing that satisfies the DUA (section 11.4). Section 6.8 covers platforms that package all of this.

8. The literature

No verified PD paper estimates two per-person latent clocks with cognitive tests loading on both. The closest work models one latent disease time (Iddi et al. 2018) or splits people into subtypes (SuStaIn studies). The worked question is therefore new, but every ingredient has a precedent to cite and learn from. Each summary below was checked against the abstract; read the full papers before quoting numbers.

8.1 The PPMI cohort and cognition

  • PPMI 2011 (Prog Neurobiol). The design paper: an observational multicentre study planned for 400 recently diagnosed PD and 200 controls, with open data and samples.

  • Marek et al. 2018 (Ann Clin Transl Neurol). Baseline of 423 PD, 196 controls and 64 SWEDD. DaTscan excluded 16% of candidate PD participants. PD showed 45% lower mean striatal and 68% lower contralateral putamen SBR than controls.

  • Weintraub et al. 2015 (Mov Disord). Baseline PPMI cognition: 22% of PD fell below the MoCA cut-off, but only 9% met test-based MCI criteria. Depression, anxiety and apathy were commoner than in controls.

  • Simuni et al. 2018 (Mov Disord). Five-year change in PPMI de novo PD. DaTscan binding fell in all striatal regions. MDS-UPDRS and DaTscan correlated weakly, and their rates of change did not correlate. Why it matters here: clinical progression and dopamine loss do not move in lockstep, which is a hint of more than one dimension, or of measurement noise.

  • Caspell-Garcia et al. 2017 (PLoS One). 423 PD followed up to 3 years, with baseline DaTscan, MRI, DTI, CSF and genotypes in mixed-effects models. Lower caudate and putamen binding, diffuse cortical loss, low CSF amyloid-beta 42 and COMT and BDNF genotypes predicted cognitive impairment. Why it matters here: dopamine imaging and structural MRI each predicted cognition independently.

  • Weintraub et al. 2025 (Mov Disord). The CSS: six tests normed against robust controls whose cognition stayed stable, then averaged. Robust norming made PD look more impaired, and processing speed and verbal memory showed the largest effects. Why it matters here: the cognitive benchmark.

  • Avila Pérez et al. 2025 (J Parkinsons Dis). 549 cognitively intact PPMI PD followed at least 5 years. Smaller one-year practice effects predicted later impairment. Why it matters here: practice effects carry signal, so model them; do not just remove them.

  • Wen et al. 2026 (Neurobiol Dis). PPMI voxel-based morphometry and free-water imaging over 4 years. Patients who developed cognitive impairment lost grey matter faster in seven regions, including thalamus and temporal lobes. Why it matters here: the strongest support for a regional S.

  • González et al. 2025 (Mov Disord). A two-page commentary on PD cognitive composites, with no abstract. Read it before citing it for any specific claim.

8.2 Theory of cognitive decline in PD

  • Kehagia, Barker & Robbins 2010 (Lancet Neurol) and 2013 (Neurodegener Dis). The dual syndrome hypothesis. Frontostriatal executive deficits follow dopamine and an inverted-U dose curve. A posterior and cholinergic syndrome, with visuospatial and memory deficits, predicts dementia.

  • Williams-Gray et al. 2007, 2009 (Brain) and 2013 (JNNP). The CamPaIGN incident cohort. Posterior-cortical tasks (semantic fluency, pentagon copying) predicted dementia; frontostriatal deficits were common but benign. Dementia reached 17% at 5 years and 46% at 10 years. COMT and MAPT genotypes mapped onto the two syndromes.

  • Litvan et al. 2012 and Emre et al. 2007 (Mov Disord). The MDS criteria for PD mild cognitive impairment and PD dementia. These are your clinical endpoints.

8.3 Dopamine imaging and cognition

  • Pasquini et al. 2019 (JNNP). 397 PPMI patients, adjusting for putamen binding. At baseline 22% already had bilateral caudate loss, which predicted more cognitive impairment, depression and worse gait over 4 years.

  • Fiorenzato et al. 2021 (Mov Disord). 249 PPMI patients with asymmetric putamen loss. Left-predominant loss went with worse attention and processing speed; right-predominant loss went with worse motor signs. Hemisphere matters.

  • Sampedro et al. 2019 (Neurobiol Dis). 87 PPMI PD with DaTscan and MRI. Lower dopamine binding went with cortical thinning, cross-sectionally and over one year. Why it matters here: D and S may be coupled, so expect a substantial correlation between the two offsets.

  • Xiang et al. 2024 (Parkinsonism Relat Disord). An event-based model in PPMI ordered left putamen, right putamen, CSF alpha-synuclein, the caudates, then neurofilament light. This is the posterior-to-anterior sequence of section 3.2, recovered from data.

  • Ruan et al. 2026 (Eur J Nucl Med Mol Imaging). In PPMI, low bilateral caudate binding predicted worse cognitive trajectories and incident MCI.

  • Ozawa et al. 2026 (NPJ Parkinsons Dis). DaTscan-based SuStaIn in 636 drug-naive PD found three subtypes: left posterior putamen, right posterior putamen and bilateral caudate. The caudate subtype had more cognitive burden. The cohort was not named in the abstract.

8.4 Structural and diffusion MRI

  • Zeighami et al. 2015 (eLife). PPMI deformation-based morphometry in 232 PD and 117 controls. Atrophy followed a network centred on the substantia nigra, consistent with spread along connections.

  • Zeighami et al. 2019 (NeuroImage Clin). That network atrophy score predicted faster motor and cognitive decline in 362 de novo PD. The area under the curve was modest (0.63) but beat other biomarkers.

  • Schulz et al. 2018 (Brain). Lower nucleus basalis volume and higher diffusivity predicted conversion to cognitive impairment.

  • Burciu et al. 2017 (Brain). Free water in the posterior substantia nigra rose over 1 and 4 years in PD, consistently across sites, and tracked putamen SBR decline.

  • Mak et al. 2015 (Brain; ICICLE-PD, not PPMI). Patients with PD-MCI showed more cortical thinning and hippocampal atrophy over 18 months.

  • Cai et al. 2025 (J Gerontol A). In 125 PPMI patients, an older-looking brain (brain-age gap) predicted conversion to MCI.

  • Sakato et al. 2024 (Brain). SuStaIn on MRI from 504 PD found three atrophy subtypes. A neocortical subtype was older at onset and declined more cognitively.

8.5 Subtypes and progression models in PD

  • Fereshtehnejad et al. 2017 (Brain). Clustering of 421 PPMI patients found three subtypes. The "diffuse malignant" one had more dopamine loss, network atrophy, an Alzheimer-like CSF profile and faster decline.

  • Lawton et al. 2018 (JNNP). Factor analysis then k-means in two UK cohorts found four replicable clusters.

  • Latourelle et al. 2017 (Lancet Neurol). Bayesian prediction of motor progression in PPMI, replicated in LABS-PD. Predicted progression could cut trial sizes by up to 20%.

  • Severson et al. 2021 (Lancet Digit Health). A hidden Markov model over 7 years of PPMI found 8 overlapping states, arguing against fixed subtypes.

  • Dadu et al. 2022 (NPJ Parkinsons Dis). Machine-learned slow, moderate and fast subtypes in PPMI, validated in PDBP. Serum NfL marked fast progression.

  • Iddi et al. 2018 (Neurodegener Dis). A latent time joint mixed-effects model in PPMI. Estimated latent time ordered participants by diagnosis without being told it, and striatal SBR and UPDRS separated stages best. Why it matters here: the one-dimensional model, the precedent the worked example extends.

  • Hähnel et al. 2024 (NPJ Parkinsons Dis). The same latent time model aligned three cohorts, then clustering found fast and slow subtypes, differing in DaTscan, gait and Alzheimer pathology.

  • Di Folco et al. 2024 (Mov Disord). Disease course mapping (the Leaspy model family) from RBD to PD. Contralateral putamen change began about 13 years before diagnosis, and cognitive decline came around diagnosis.

  • Zhou et al. 2023 (NPJ Parkinsons Dis). SuStaIn on clinical and diffusion and neuromelanin MRI found two trajectories: one starting with RBD, autonomic and brainstem changes, the other with smell loss, cognition and limbic changes.

  • Oxtoby et al. 2021 (Brain). An event-based model with PPMI as external cohort. Smell and sleep came first, then visual cognition and brain iron, then nigral and cortical degeneration.

  • Poulet & Durrleman 2023 (Stat Med). Leaspy extended to ordinal item scores and applied to PPMI.

  • Brumm et al. 2023 (J Parkinsons Dis). 25 progression milestones across six domains in PPMI. Lower DaTscan binding predicted reaching them sooner; starting treatment did not.

8.6 Methods borrowed from Alzheimer's research

  • Jedynak et al. 2012 and Donohue et al. 2014: a progression score that places each person on a shared timeline; long-term curves from short follow-ups.

  • Li et al. 2019: the Bayesian latent time joint mixed-effects model, tested on simulations and ADNI.

  • Schiratti et al. 2017: mixed-effects trajectories with person-specific time shift and speed, the basis of Leaspy.

  • Lorenzi et al. 2019: Gaussian process progression models with uncertainty.

  • Fonteijn et al. 2012; Young et al. 2014, 2018: event-based models and SuStaIn.

  • Young et al. 2024 (Nat Rev Neurosci): the best overview of the whole field. Read it first.

8.7 Biological definitions and staging

  • Siderowf et al. 2023 (Lancet Neurol). CSF seed amplification in 1,123 PPMI participants: high sensitivity and specificity for PD, with real molecular heterogeneity.

  • Simuni et al. 2024 (Lancet Neurol). NSD-ISS: staging anchored by S (synuclein) and D (dopamine dysfunction) plus functional impairment.

  • Höglinger et al. 2024 (Lancet Neurol). SynNeurGe: S, N (neurodegeneration on imaging) and G (genes). Its N component is close in spirit to S.

  • Recent reviews: Bougea 2025 (staging and markers), Hatano et al. 2026 (alpha-synuclein biomarkers and staging), and Mulick et al. 2026 (progression models in moderate PD).

9. Strengthening the worked example

The idea is good: a falsifiable, prediction-based test of one versus two progression dimensions, grounded in the dual syndrome hypothesis. Three things threaten a project like it: a weak structural anchor, counts without written derivations, and open questions about treatment and identifiability. All three can be settled in the first two months.

9.1 What is strong

  • A falsifiable question with a publishable null. "One dimension is enough" is a useful answer too.

  • Comparison on held-out participants. This avoids the usual trap of a more complex model winning in-sample.

  • Tests are not assigned in advance, and cognition does not choose the MRI regions. This avoids circular reasoning.

  • Biologically motivated anchors with a literature behind them: latent time models (Iddi et al. 2018) and the dual syndrome account (section 3.5).

  • Real dates instead of nominal visits, which respects PPMI's mismatched schedules.

  • An honest caveat: two statistical dimensions are not automatically two mechanisms.

  • Working infrastructure: this repository's pipeline, SQL interface and CSS scoring.

9.3 Risks in the science itself

  • DaTScan floors. Putamen binding nears its floor within about four years of diagnosis (section 3.1), so D is measured worse later on. Use the caudate, age- and sex-adjusted values, and a curve that flattens.

  • S may just be ageing. Total grey matter shrinks with age in everyone. Without controls' trajectories or an age model, S can absorb normal ageing and look like a disease dimension.

  • A second dimension might be a measurement artefact. If the two-dimensional model wins only because DaTScan follows its own course, that shows a modality-specific trajectory, not a second disease process. To argue for two processes you need more:

    • The cognitive loadings split as predicted.

    • The dissociation is double: D predicts motor outcomes and S predicts cognitive ones.

    • The pattern replicates. Borsboom, Mellenbergh and van Heerden 2003, on the theoretical status of latent variables, is the reading for this argument.

  • Heterogeneity can masquerade as dimensionality. Subgroups (for example GBA carriers or SAA-negative LRRK2 carriers) moving at different speeds can mimic a second dimension. Run sensitivity analyses that exclude them, and know how SuStaIn-style subtyping differs from the worked question (section 10.4).

9.4 How to sharpen it

  1. One primary comparison, one rule. For example: "Two correlated latent stages predict held-out participants' later DaTScan, MRI and cognitive measurements better than one shared stage. The paired held-out log-likelihood difference has a 95% bootstrap interval that excludes zero."

  2. A directional secondary hypothesis. Letter-Number Sequencing and Symbol Digit load more on D; fluency, line orientation and delayed recall load more on S. Stating the direction in advance makes it a fair test.

  3. A double dissociation as validation. D predicts motor milestones better; S predicts cognitive milestones better.

  4. Connect to biological staging. PPMI's NSD-ISS already uses DaTScan as its D anchor (section 2.2). Asking whether a second, structural axis adds to D is timely and easy to explain.

  5. Use every participant. Fit the joint model on everyone with any of the three modalities. The overlap only needs to be large enough to estimate the link between D and S.

  6. Run the power simulation first (section 10.4). It tells you the sample size and which offset model you can actually fit.

10. Statistics and modelling you need

You need five layers: regression thinking, mixed models for repeated measures, latent variable models, disease-progression (latent time) models, and honest out-of-sample validation. The worked example's model sits on all five. Learn them in this order.

10.1 Foundations

  • Likelihood. A model assigns a probability to the data. Fitting chooses parameters that make the data most probable. Held-out log-likelihood, the worked example's primary metric, is the same quantity evaluated on participants the model never saw.

  • Bayesian inference. Combine a prior with the likelihood to get a posterior distribution over parameters. It is natural for hierarchical models with many person-level parameters, and it gives uncertainty for each person's stage.

  • Regression. Interpret coefficients, interactions and splines for curves, and check residuals. Generalised linear models handle bounded or count outcomes: binomial for line orientation, ordinal for rating scales.

  • Uncertainty. Distinguish confidence intervals from prediction intervals. Use the bootstrap, resampling participants rather than visits.

  • Multiple comparisons. Many secondary tests need false discovery rate control (Benjamini & Hochberg 1995).

10.2 Longitudinal data and mixed-effects models

Repeated measurements on one person are correlated, so ordinary regression understates uncertainty. A linear mixed-effects model (Laird & Ware 1982) gives each person their own intercept and slope around the population average:

y_{ij} = (\beta_0 + b_{0i}) + (\beta_1 + b_{1i})\, t_{ij} + \varepsilon_{ij}, \qquad (b_{0i}, b_{1i}) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma), \quad \varepsilon_{ij} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)

Here y is a test score for person i at visit j, t is time, the betas are population effects and the b terms are person-specific deviations.

  • Time-varying covariates such as LEDD change within a person. Separate their within-person and between-person effects by centring.

  • Missing data (Rubin 1976) comes in three kinds:

    • Missing completely at random (MCAR): missingness unrelated to anything.

    • Missing at random (MAR): missingness explained by observed data.

    • Missing not at random (MNAR): missingness depends on the unobserved values themselves.

  • What that means here. Likelihood-based mixed models are valid under MAR. PPMI dropout after decline is probably MNAR. Check it with pattern-mixture analyses or a joint model of the trajectories and dropout (Rizopoulos 2012). Multiple imputation (van Buuren & Groothuis-Oudshoorn 2011) suits missing covariates.

10.3 Latent variable models

A latent variable is an unobserved quantity measured by several noisy indicators. Each indicator has a loading (how strongly it reflects the latent variable) and its own residual noise.

  • Factor analysis. Exploratory analysis asks how many factors the data need. Confirmatory analysis tests a specified structure using fit indices such as CFI and RMSEA. A single-factor model of the six CSS tests is exactly what the worked question challenges.

  • Identification. Latent scales are arbitrary. Fix each latent variable's scale and sign, for example by anchoring D to DaTScan and S to MRI. Without anchors, a two-factor model can be rotated freely and the names "dopaminergic" and "structural" mean nothing.

  • Measurement invariance. For latent scores to be comparable over time or across groups, loadings and intercepts must be stable. Test this before interpreting change.

  • Longitudinal latent models. Latent growth curves and structural equation models are available in lavaan (R) and semopy (Python). Item response theory handles item-level data and ceilings.

The two-dimension model is a longitudinal two-factor model in which each factor is a person's position on a disease clock.

10.4 Disease progression models

These models put each person's observations on a shared disease timeline by estimating where that person sits on it.

Latent time-shift models. Each person gets an offset (and sometimes a rate) that slides their data along a common set of biomarker curves (Jedynak et al. 2012; Donohue et al. 2014). Iddi et al. 2018 applied this to PPMI, the closest precedent for the worked example. Related tools: the Bayesian latent time joint mixed-effects model (Li et al. 2019), Leaspy's logistic curves (Schiratti et al. 2017; Poulet & Durrleman 2023, also on PPMI) and Gaussian process progression models (Lorenzi et al. 2019).

The two-dimension model, written out. One illustrative specification:

\begin{aligned}
D_i(t) &= t + \delta_i^{D}, \qquad S_i(t) = t + \delta_i^{S}, \qquad (\delta_i^{D}, \delta_i^{S}) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Omega) \\
\mathrm{SBR}_{ir}(t) &= f_r\!\left(D_i(t)\right) + \varepsilon_{irt}, \qquad r \in \{\text{left, right}\} \times \{\text{caudate, putamen}\} \\
\mathrm{MRI}_{im}(t) &= g_m\!\left(S_i(t)\right) + \varepsilon_{imt} \\
y_{ik}(t) &= \alpha_k + \lambda_{kD}\, D_i(t) + \lambda_{kS}\, S_i(t) + \beta_k^{\top} x_{ik}(t) + \varepsilon_{ikt}, \qquad k = 1, \dots, 6
\end{aligned}

In words:

  • Each person has two offsets, one per clock.

  • The DaTScan regions follow curves f in D, and the MRI measures follow curves g in S.

  • Each cognitive test k mixes D and S through loadings lambda, plus covariates x (age, sex, education, practice, LEDD).

  • The one-stage model sets the two offsets equal, so a single clock drives everything.

  • A key choice is one offset or two. Here it is two, correlated through Omega, compared with one.

Event-based models and SuStaIn. These order biomarker "events" (becoming abnormal) from mostly cross-sectional data (Fonteijn et al. 2012; Young et al. 2014). Oxtoby et al. 2021 applied it to PD, with PPMI as an external cohort. SuStaIn adds subtypes, groups of people who follow different sequences (Young et al. 2018). Keep this distinction clear in every talk you give:

  • The worked question: do two dimensions progress within each person?

  • SuStaIn's question: are there two kinds of people?

State models. Hidden Markov models assign people to discrete disease states over time (Severson et al. 2021, on PPMI).

Identifiability, the practical trap. With two to four observations per modality per person, offsets are estimable. Person-specific rates on two clocks mostly are not. Offsets and rates trade off, and so do curve shapes and offsets. Use hierarchical shrinkage priors, fix rates or give them strong priors, and centre the offsets.

Simulate before you fit real data. Generate fake data from the one-stage and two-stage models with PPMI's real visit schedule and sample size. Fit both models to each and ask two questions. Do the parameters come back? Can the comparison tell the two models apart? This is your power analysis, and it can be done entirely on synthetic data, outside the DUA.

10.5 Model comparison and validation

  • The metric. Use held-out log predictive density per participant (negative log-likelihood, lower is better). Report error and the coverage of predictive intervals as support.

  • The split. Cross-validate at the participant level, with folds of participants, never of visits. Do model tuning in an inner loop: nested cross-validation (Varma & Simon 2006; Cawley & Talbot 2010).

  • Landmarking. Estimate held-out participants' stages from data up to about 2.5 years and score their later data (van Houwelingen 2007).

  • Bayesian shortcuts. PSIS-LOO (Vehtari et al. 2017) is cheap, but leave out whole participants, not single observations.

  • The decision. Compute paired per-participant differences in held-out log-likelihood between the models. Bootstrap them over participants and pre-register the threshold. A more flexible model can win on training data and still lose here; that is the point.

  • Reporting. Follow TRIPOD+AI for prediction models (Collins et al. 2024) and STROBE for observational studies (von Elm et al. 2007).

10.6 Causal thinking, just enough

The worked example is predictive and descriptive, not causal; say so in any paper. Three traps still apply:

  • Confounding by indication. Faster progressors get more medication, so LEDD tracks severity as well as treatment.

  • Selection. PPMI volunteers are highly educated and mostly white, and dropout is selective.

  • Adjusting for mediators. Controlling for a variable on the causal path, such as motor severity when studying SDMT, can remove real signal.

Draw a causal diagram (DAG) before choosing covariates (Hernán & Robins 2020).

11.2 Reproducibility

  • Version control. Keep all code in git. Use notebooks to explore and present; put logic in modules, as the pipeline does.

  • Frozen inputs. Record the IDA download date and file hashes. data.PPMI reads each table's download date from its file name, clean.source_manifest names the file behind each clean table, and a checksum such as shasum -a 256 ~/ppmi/study/* records the rest. Never edit a raw CSV by hand.

  • Pinned environment. Use requirements.txt with versions. Record imaging software versions too: different FreeSurfer versions give different volumes.

  • Seeds and splits. Fix random seeds. Save the cross-validation fold assignment inside the approved environment so anyone can rerun the same folds.

  • Tests. Test every transformation on a synthetic fixture against an independent re-implementation. This is how the pipeline was checked, and it catches the bugs that silently change counts.

  • Pre-registration. Before fitting real data, write down the cohort, variables, models, metric, sensitivity analyses and decision rule. Timestamp it publicly (for example on OSF) or at least in git.

11.3 Validation hygiene

  • Split by participant and tune inside folds. Fit all preprocessing within each training fold.

  • Compare against simple baselines. The two-stage model must beat more than the one-stage model:

    • A population-average trajectory.

    • A CSS-slope model.

    • A DaTScan-only model.

    • Last observation carried forward.

  • Report variation across folds and bootstrap intervals, not one number.

  • Pre-list the sensitivity analyses: SDMT dropped or motor-adjusted, complete cases only, Xing-only DaTScan, genetic PD excluded, SAA-negative excluded.

  • Temporal validation. If the sample allows, fit on participants enrolled before 2020 and test on those enrolled after. It is stricter than random folds, because protocol and population changed between the phases.

11.4 Working under the DUA

  • Who. Everyone who touches participant-level data must be named on the PPMI data use application and accept the DUA. That includes every collaborator. Outside reviewers can see code, methods and aggregate results without registering, but not rows.

  • Where. Use the approved environment only. Do not upload data to platforms that keep or train on user content. brainlife.io, for example, keeps archived datasets permanently (section 6.8). Institutional computing that does not share data is fine.

  • AI tools. Use them freely on code, documentation, synthetic data and published papers. Never on participant rows, DuckDB files or notebook outputs that show IDs. This guide and the repository's code were written under that rule, from code, synthetic fixtures and published documents, never participant data.

  • What can leave. Aggregate tables, population curves and model summaries can leave. Per-participant plots and tables stay inside.

  • Before publishing. Allow two weeks for the PPMI committee's administrative review. Release code without data.

11.5 Working together

  • A decision log. Keep a table of date, decision, reason and owner. Most contradictions in a project come from undocumented choices.

  • One source of truth for counts: one dated notebook output, such as notebooks/audit.ipynb. Nobody quotes a number that is not in it.

  • Two-person rule. Before a number goes into a slide or the paper, a second person runs the notebooks from scratch and gets the same result.

  • Short weekly written updates. Each covers what changed, what was decided and what is blocked. They become the methods section later.

12. Running a PPMI project

A project like the worked example needs a few early decisions that unblock everyone, protection from scope creep, and a clear owner for each problem. The plan below fits six months of part-time work, with a gate at the end of each phase where you either proceed or simplify.

12.1 The plan by phase

  1. Month 1: define.

    • Freeze the cohort, time window, endpoints, D and S measures and test variables.

    • Derive every count with written definitions in one notebook; notebooks/audit.ipynb is the start.

    • Run notebooks/atlas.ipynb to download the missing tables: LEDD, MDS-UPDRS I to IV, the Curated Data Cut, SAA status and genetic status.

    • Choose the S option.

    • Gate 1: a signed-off analysis plan and one cohort table.

  2. Month 2: simulate and prepare.

    • Run the identifiability and power simulation on synthetic data.

    • Reproduce the CSS benchmark (code/ppmi_pipeline/css.py and audit.ipynb already score it) and fit the simple baselines.

    • If S needs raw T1, start quality control and segmentation now (sections 6.6 to 6.8).

    • Gate 2: the simulation shows the one-versus-two comparison is decidable at the available sample size. If not, simplify: fixed rates, fewer curves, or pairwise models.

  3. Months 3 and 4: fit and compare.

    • Fit both models in nested participant-level cross-validation with the 2.5-year landmark.

    • Estimate the cognitive loadings.

    • Run the pre-listed sensitivity analyses.

    • Gate 3: apply the decision rule and lock the primary result before any secondary exploration.

  4. Month 5: validate and interpret.

    • Test whether D predicts motor milestones and S cognitive ones.

    • Compare with NSD-ISS stage.

    • Run exploratory genetics.

    • Gate 4: a clinician reviews the biology and a second reader tests the claims.

  5. Month 6: write and release.

    • Draft the paper with figures and released code.

    • Submit to PPMI's committee two weeks before the journal, then post a preprint.

    • Gate 5: committee clearance, then submission.

12.2 Questions to settle

  • Which clinical endpoints, and is genetic PD in or out of the primary analysis?

  • Which computing is approved under the DUA for MRI processing?

  • Can the raw T1 scans be processed and checked in the time available?

  • Are the rules behind every count written down and reproduced by one notebook?

  • Draft the limits-of-interpretation paragraph and the criteria for naming the dimensions.

  • Ask an outside reviewer to check the analysis plan and the simulation design.

  • Agree one vocabulary (one-dimensional vs two-dimensional; D and S) and use it everywhere.

  • Choose the window (5 or 5.5 years) and the time origin, and log the decision.
